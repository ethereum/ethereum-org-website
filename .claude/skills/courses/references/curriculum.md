# Curriculum

The canonical home for course order, vocabulary rules and the term ledger.

## Order is a suggestion

`TRACKS` in `src/data/courses/index.ts` groups courses into hub sections (track, then courses, then lessons). `COURSES` flattens them in order and is the suggested path; the hub lists each track's courses in that order. Nothing is locked: learners can open any course or lesson. Lessons in a course are still written to be taken in order.

Suggested path: **Everyday → Wallets → Blockchains → Ethereum → Sending → Smart contracts** (essentials, beginner), then **Tokens → NFTs → Layer 2 → Trading and lending → Scams** (using Ethereum, beginner), then **Gas → Proof of stake → Rollups → ETH → Upgrades → Smart accounts** (going deeper, intermediate). The advanced track (under the hood) has teasers only. Everyday is a first-day tour for someone who has never used crypto: it touches every later course's main idea at intro depth through everyday objects, with no mechanics, so the later courses are each a deeper second pass (they still restate terms in a clause, since nothing is locked). Wallets is the friendly hook and needs only a one-line idea of a blockchain. Blockchains explains any blockchain: hashes, blocks, nodes, agreeing on the next page (proof of work and proof of stake in one sentence each) and why there are many blockchains. Ethereum then shows what Ethereum does with one: tracking more than money, programs anyone can add (every node runs them the same way), ETH's three jobs, who runs it and what people use it for. Sending follows a transaction from signing to the block explorer, with no numbers: what the fee pays for, waiting in line for a block (tips), and failed payments. Smart contracts closes the essentials: a contract's own address and code, reading vs writing, tokens as a contract's list, verified code and approvals. The using track puts those to work, still with no numbers: Tokens (contract addresses, stablecoins and their backing, sending on the right network, unasked tokens as bait), NFTs (one-of-a-kind tokens, where the picture lives, copies, Ethereum names, approve all), Layer 2 (express lanes, bundles, networks, bridges), Trading and lending (pools, price limits, collateral, who pays a reward) and Scams (the four marks, copycat sites, permission signatures, where bait leads, what to do after). The intermediate courses revisit the same ground with numbers, ordered so terms come before use: Gas (working out fees, base fee and burn, block space, failed and stuck transactions), Proof of stake (validators, voting, slots and epochs, finality times, penalties and slashing), Rollups (posted data and blobs, optimistic and ZK proofs, exits, bridge risk; it leans on layer 2 and gas), ETH (native balances, gwei, supply, wrapping, staking; it leans on gas and proof of stake), Upgrades (governance, EIPs, clients, forks) and Smart accounts (account rules, sponsored fees, EIP-7702 delegation).

Each track's level limits its copy (see the levels table in `SKILL.md`): a beginner lesson that needs a number or a timing belongs in an intermediate course instead.

**Everyday** follows its own lesson pattern instead of the anatomy in `authoring.md`: a picture card with an everyday scene and no crypto words, a try-it step on that everyday version, an "In Ethereum, that's..." card naming the term, one question from the everyday version to the real one, then the takeaway. Cards stay at two short sentences, with no numbers or statistics. Its exam is single-answer choices only (the engine has no match questions in exams), mostly "what is the Ethereum twin of this everyday object".

## Vocabulary rules

1. **Introduce, then assume.** A term is introduced when a concept step defines it in plain words (bold it with `<strong>` there). After that, later lessons in the same course use it freely.
2. **Define on first use when no earlier lesson did.** Use a short appositive ("a <strong>gas limit</strong>: the most gas the transaction is allowed to use") rather than a separate lesson. Prompts and options are plain text, so define the term in a concept step or in the prompt sentence itself.
3. **Across courses, assume earlier courses on the suggested path, but add a short reminder.** Because nothing is locked, a lesson that leans on an earlier course's term restates it in a clause ("Validators, who stake ETH to run the network, …"). Never write "as you saw in course X".
4. **Prefer plain description to a new term.** If a term is used only once, describe it instead ("the same turn", not "the same slot", until a lesson needs slots for real, as finality does). Every new term costs the learner attention.
5. **Preview, don't presume.** When a step has to touch a topic no lesson covers yet, describe it plainly, name it once, and flag it: "We'll learn more about slashing in a future lesson." Then add it to the owed topics below. Never use an uncovered term as if the learner knows it, and don't name later topics just to sound complete.
6. **Feedback and widget strings count.** Option feedback, explains and widget labels follow the same rules as concept text.

## Spiral depth

A topic can appear several times at increasing depth: a simplified first pass that avoids jargon, then a later lesson or course that revisits it once its dependencies exist. Depth levels used in the ledger:

- **intro**: plain-language idea, no mechanics ("a shared record that thousands of computers keep in sync").
- **core**: how it works, with the term in play ("blocks linked by hashes").
- **deep**: edge cases, numbers and trade-offs ("finality after about 13 to 19 minutes; a third of stake can stall it").

A later pass may deepen a term but must not contradict the earlier simplification. Tighten it ("roughly", "usually") rather than leave a wrong mental model.

Simplifications must stay true. An address is "safe to share" only in that nobody can spend from it: anyone who has it can see its balance and history, so never call it safe without that caveat nearby.
